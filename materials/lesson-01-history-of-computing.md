# Lesson 1: History of Computing

## The Mechanical Era

Long before electronics, people built mechanical devices to automate calculation. The
abacus, used for thousands of years, helped merchants perform arithmetic. In the 1830s,
Charles Babbage designed the Analytical Engine, a mechanical general-purpose computer
that was never completed in his lifetime. Ada Lovelace, who wrote extensive notes on
the Analytical Engine, described how it could follow a sequence of instructions and is
often called the first computer programmer.

## Theoretical Foundations

In 1936, Alan Turing published a paper describing a simple abstract machine — now
called a Turing machine — that could carry out any computation that can be described
as a sequence of steps. This idea defined what it means for a problem to be computable
and remains the theoretical foundation of computer science. Turing also made major
contributions to codebreaking at Bletchley Park during World War II.

## The Electronic Era

ENIAC, completed in 1945 at the University of Pennsylvania, is generally considered the
first general-purpose electronic digital computer. It used about 18,000 vacuum tubes,
weighed 30 tons, and was programmed by physically rewiring connections. Vacuum tubes
were unreliable and generated enormous heat, which limited how large computers could
practically become.

## The Transistor and the Integrated Circuit

The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, replaced the vacuum tube. Transistors
are smaller, faster, cheaper, and far more reliable. In 1958, Jack Kilby and Robert
Noyce independently developed the integrated circuit, which places many transistors on
a single chip of silicon. Integrated circuits made it possible to shrink a room-sized
computer onto a desk.

## The Microprocessor and Personal Computing

In 1971, Intel released the 4004, the first commercial microprocessor — an entire
central processing unit on one chip. Microprocessors made personal computers possible.
The Apple II (1977) and the IBM PC (1981) brought computing into homes and offices.
Graphical user interfaces, popularized by the Apple Macintosh in 1984, replaced typed
commands with windows, icons, and a mouse.

## The Internet Age

The ARPANET, first connected in 1969, linked university and government computers and
evolved into the modern internet. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989
at CERN, combining hypertext with the internet to create linked pages that anyone could
publish and browse. Search engines, smartphones, and cloud computing built on these
foundations to make computing ubiquitous.
